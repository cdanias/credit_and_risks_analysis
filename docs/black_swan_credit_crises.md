# Prominent Black Swan Credit Crises Since 1952

Here is a chronological list of the most prominent "Black Swan" events that have triggered severe credit crises since 1952, including their primary drivers, secondary drivers, and the ultimate stimulus mechanisms used to restore market stability.

### 1. The Herstatt Bank Failure & Oil Shock (1974)
*   **Primary Driver:** The 1973 OPEC oil embargo caused oil prices to quadruple overnight, leading to massive global stagflation and economic shock.
*   **Secondary Driver:** Amidst the chaos of newly floating exchange rates, the German Bankhaus Herstatt made disastrous, highly leveraged foreign exchange bets. When German regulators closed the bank at the end of the German business day, US banks had paid Deutsche Marks but not yet received their US Dollars. This created massive counterparty credit risk (now known as "Herstatt Risk"), instantly freezing the global interbank lending market.
*   **Stimulus / Recovery Mechanism:** Central banks had to step in as lenders of last resort to domestic banks. More importantly, this crisis birthed the **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS)**, fundamentally changing how cross-border credit risk and settlements are regulated to this day.

### 2. The Latin American Debt Crisis (1982)
*   **Primary Driver:** In the 1970s, Latin American countries borrowed heavily from US commercial banks. In the early 1980s, Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker aggressively raised US interest rates to double digits to fight inflation.
*   **Secondary Driver:** The resulting global recession caused commodity prices to collapse. Latin American countries lost their export revenue exactly when the interest on their dollar-denominated debt skyrocketed, leading Mexico (and subsequently others) to declare they could not service their debt, creating a massive sovereign credit crisis for US banks.
*   **Stimulus / Recovery Mechanism:** Initially, the IMF provided emergency bailout loans conditional on strict structural adjustment programs. The ultimate market recovery came via the creation of **"Brady Bonds" (1989)**—where US banks agreed to forgive some debt in exchange for new tradable bonds that were collateralized by US Treasury zero-coupon bonds, effectively removing the toxic credit risk from bank balance sheets.

### 3. Russian Default & LTCM Collapse (1998)
*   **Primary Driver:** Following the Asian Financial Crisis, collapsing oil prices and severe structural economic issues caused the Russian government to completely default on its domestic debt (GKO bonds) and devalue the ruble.
*   **Secondary Driver:** The default triggered a massive global "flight to quality." Investors dumped risky credit assets and bought safe-haven US Treasuries. This dynamic completely blew up the highly leveraged arbitrage models of **Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM)**, a massive hedge fund. LTCM’s impending collapse threatened a domino effect of counterparty credit defaults across every major Wall Street bank.
*   **Stimulus / Recovery Mechanism:** Instead of using taxpayer money, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York locked the heads of the major Wall Street banks in a room and orchestrated a **$3.6 billion private bailout/takeover** of LTCM. Simultaneously, the Fed cut interest rates to inject liquidity and calm credit markets.

### 4. The Global Financial Crisis (2007-2008)
*   **Primary Driver:** The collapse of the US subprime mortgage market. Predatory lending, artificially low interest rates, and the assumption that housing prices would never fall created a massive asset bubble.
*   **Secondary Driver:** The securitization of these mortgages into complex structured credit products (CDOs and MBS). Because the credit risk was sliced, diced, and hidden behind AAA ratings, when defaults spiked, banks stopped lending to each other entirely because nobody knew who was holding the toxic assets. This peaked with the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers.
*   **Stimulus / Recovery Mechanism:** Unprecedented central bank intervention. The US Treasury launched the **Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP)** to directly inject capital into failing banks. Central banks globally slashed interest rates to near zero (ZIRP) and initiated **Quantitative Easing (QE)**—printing trillions to buy mortgage-backed securities and government bonds to artificially suppress yields and force liquidity back into credit markets.

### 5. The European Sovereign Debt Crisis (2011-2012)
*   **Primary Driver:** Following the GFC, it was revealed that several European nations (notably Greece) had massive, unsustainable, and previously hidden sovereign debt. 
*   **Secondary Driver:** The "Doom Loop." European banks held massive amounts of their own governments' sovereign bonds. As government credit ratings were downgraded (increasing the probability of default), bank balance sheets were devastated, which in turn required governments to borrow *more* money to bail out the banks, further degrading sovereign credit.
*   **Stimulus / Recovery Mechanism:** Institutional rescue packages from the "Troika" (the EU, ECB, and IMF). However, the ultimate circuit breaker was European Central Bank President Mario Draghi’s famous 2012 pledge to do **"whatever it takes"** to preserve the Euro. The ECB launched Long-Term Refinancing Operations (LTROs) and Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT) to act as a backstop buyer of sovereign debt, immediately crushing credit spreads and stabilizing the market.

### 6. The COVID-19 Liquidity & Credit Crunch (March 2020)
*   **Primary Driver:** The sudden, global, government-mandated economic shutdown to curb the pandemic, causing an instant halt to corporate revenues.
*   **Secondary Driver:** A brutal "dash for cash." Investors panicked, assuming widespread corporate defaults were imminent. They sold absolutely everything (corporate credit, equities, and even safe-haven Treasuries) to raise cash. The plumbing of the financial system seized up, and corporate credit spreads exploded.
*   **Stimulus / Recovery Mechanism:** The fastest and largest monetary and fiscal intervention in human history. Crucially for credit markets, the Federal Reserve crossed a historic red line by creating the **Primary and Secondary Market Corporate Credit Facilities**. The Fed literally promised to buy corporate bonds (and even some "junk" high-yield bonds and ETFs). Just the *announcement* of this backstop was enough to instantly reopen credit markets and restore pricing stability.
